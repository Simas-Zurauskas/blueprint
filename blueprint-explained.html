Skill version 2

The Blueprint, explained

A product definition built from whatever exists — a client's material, a team's notes, or nothing but your own idea — grilled until the gray areas are named, and locked once it is settled. Every gap in it is said out loud rather than guessed at.

One

What it is, and what it is for

A project starts as a pile of material nobody has reconciled: a deck, a call transcript, two pages of notes, an email thread, and whatever three people remember differently. Somewhere in it is a product. Also in it: two sources that contradict each other, four decisions nobody wrote down, and a dozen things that were never decided at all and will therefore be decided by whoever writes the code first.

The Blueprint is what you get when that pile — or just the idea in your head — is turned into a definition you can hand to whoever builds it, human or agent, and to whoever has to agree to it. When it is settled you lock it, and from then on every change is one readable entry in a change log.

The one thing it will not do

Guess. Every sentence traces to a source somebody can point at, or it is an admitted gap carrying a marker and a question with a named owner. An unsupported sentence in a specification is worse than an admitted gap: a gap gets asked about, a guess gets built.

Everything else in this page is machinery for keeping that true while still being useful. Four things should hold when it is done:

  • You can build from it. Hand a feature row to a developer or an agent and what comes back is what you meant — including what you did not want built.
  • It says what nobody has decided, in a list with names against it, instead of quietly inventing an answer.
  • It settles whether a new idea belongs, by citation rather than argument.
  • What moved since we settled it is always one page. Every post-lock change carries the ask in the words of whoever asked.

It records intent, never implementation. Nothing here looks at a running app, reads a repo, or tracks work in flight. That boundary is deliberate: a document that tries to be both a definition and a status report is reliably neither. The document may keep growing after the lock — ideas do — but every change is deliberate, gated, and logged.

Worth it when somebody outside your head has to build this, or pay for it, or agree to it — and "somebody" includes a coding agent, and includes you in three months. Not worth it for a throwaway prototype.

Two

What you actually do

Seven steps, and only three of them are yours to think hard about.

1 · Give it whatever you already have

/blueprint init, and hand over the material however it is — a deck, a transcript, a page of notes, three contradictory documents, or nothing but yourself and ten minutes. It reads everything verbatim first, then shows you a proposed skeleton: the features it found, the exclusions, the contradictions, the gaps, and what it did not use and why. Then it stops. Nothing is created until you confirm, and declining is a normal ending.

One shape of source it refuses: a code repository. What the product should do is not recoverable from what somebody already built — "the code does it, so that is what we meant" is the most common way a document records a bug as a requirement. Where a project is half-built, describe the behaviour you want kept, in words, and that is a source like any other.

2 · It grills the document; you decide which finds are real

The part that earns the tool, and it is never skipped — the skeleton at init, every delta at add, the whole document before a lock. Covered on its own, next.

3 · You answer the ones you kept

Write the answer and why, put your name in Confirmed by, and move Confirmed to Human approved. A row still reading AI generated is never applied, however good the answer looks — that is the whole job of the label.

4 · /blueprint resolve writes the answers in

For each vetted answer, one agent drafts the change and a second, independent one checks it against the answer — asked where is the inconsistency, never do you agree. Then it writes into the feature the question touches, removes the marker, and dates the provenance line. Three outcomes, and the third is the honest one: Clean, Patched, or Flagged — we tried and could not write this honestly, with the objection on the row.

5 · You agree the features

Set Intent = Agreed yourself, and put your name to it. No run ever does either.

6 · /blueprint lock

It prints a readiness report, runs one final grilling over the whole document, and you acknowledge each unsettled item on the record. Then the Blueprint is locked.

7 · After that, every change writes the change log

Locking halts nothing — the document stays live and every change still runs the same gates. What changes is the record: one entry per change, with the ask in the words of whoever asked.

/blueprint add takes more material in at any point, locked or not; /blueprint status reads everything and writes nothing.

Three

The grilling, and why a proposal is not a question

Nothing planned is ever written or locked without being adversarially attacked first. The drafted skeleton at init, every delta at add, the whole document one last time before a lock, and any time you ask — "grill this spec". Four hostile lenses, each run as its own pass:

  • The builder who must not guess — walks each feature as if implementing it tomorrow, forbidden from deciding anything. Every place they would have to stop and ask is a find. This lens reads the document the way its real consumer will.
  • The hostile tester — attacks each requirement, hunting for an input or a sequence the sentence does not decide. Its sharpest tool: draft both behaviours a requirement could mean and compare — where the drafts diverge, the divergence names the question.
  • The first week of real life — data lifecycle, permissions, empty and error states, anything touching money, what exists on day one.
  • Collisions and boundaries — two features touching the same record with nothing saying who wins; edges where a feature meets another feature, a third party, or money.

What the grilling produces are proposals. They land at Status = Proposed, invisible to every open-questions view, each carrying a Why asked line naming what prompted it. Then it asks you, one row at a time:

PROPOSED (6) — none of these is a question yet. Ordered by how much the answer changes.

  1/6  «Can a customer retry a failed payment?»
       why asked: «Checkout» FR-2 says payment succeeds or fails; no source says what
                  happens next, and no edge case covers it
       a marker on «Checkout» is waiting on this — rejecting it decides the marker too
       suggested owner: Ana
       [a]pprove · a[n]swer now · [e]dit · [r]eject · already [d]ecided · [s]kip

Only what you approve becomes a real question. Rejections keep their reason in the record and never reappear as unresolved product questions. Skipping is not rejecting — a skipped proposal comes back next sitting.

And if you can answer on the spot, say so. Answer now takes your answer in your own words, verbatim, drafts the row, and leaves exactly one act for later — your name in Confirmed by, which no run may ever fill. One sitting from gray area to closed, with the one click that keeps the provenance honest.

Why the extra step

A generated question that lands straight in the list acquires the authority of one somebody actually asked. The list then reads as your real unknowns while holding a machine's guesses about what might be unknown — and the measured ceiling on that generator is low. The best agent on the nearest benchmark finds 44.4% of real gaps. Most of what it produces is noise, and an empty proposal list is never evidence the Blueprint is complete.

About ten a sitting, ordered by how much the answer changes what gets built and by whether the named owner can actually answer it. Past ten, the rest are named as next sitting's — the cap binds what the run offers, never what you may pull forward. Past that number people stop reading and start agreeing, which is worse than a shorter list.

Rejecting takes a reason, and the reason decides something

This is the subtlest part of the design. A proposal is often raised over a [NEEDS CLARIFICATION] marker sitting in a feature body. What happens to that marker depends on why you rejected the question:

You sayThe marker
Not a real gapRemoved, citing the rejected row — it was raised over nothing
Already decidedRemoved, citing the requirement or Not doing line that answers it
Ask it betterStays. The gap is real; only the wording was wrong

Without that split there are two ways to be wrong and no way to be right. Remove the marker on every rejection and a badly-worded question quietly converts a known unknown into an unknown unknown. Remove it on none and a rejected proposal strands its marker forever — blocking that feature from ever being agreed, with nothing in the system able to clear it, so the definition can never honestly be settled.

You can also do all of this in Notion instead, at your own pace: proposals are ordinary rows in the Proposed — needs review view. Approve or reject them there and the next run reads what you did and never reverses it.

Four

The shape

An overview page, two databases, an append-only run log, and — once locked — the change log. There is no third database and nothing tracks work.

Day one the whole thing is an overview page and two empty databases. A one-paragraph document with nine open questions is valid and honest.

The overview never grows: every human block is capped and every index on it is a view, so it is the same size at 200 features as on day one. Its blocks are a TL;DR, What this product is ending in a one-sentence NOT-clause, Who it's for, one picture, three ⟳ views, links, and an Operating block. A ⟳ heading is a view; text typed under one is an error the next run reports.

A run may write an overview block, but only as verbatim text you accept at a checkpoint — one block per write, never silently. The overview is part of what is being drafted here, and a NOT-clause that no longer matches the features is a defect in the thing people will build from.

Five

A feature row is the spec

There is no second feature document. The row's body has five blocks:

BlockWhat goes in it
## WhyTwo to four sentences: the problem, who has it, why now. No solution talk.
## BehaviourNumbered requirements, FR-1 onward, each one a thing that can fail.
## Edge casesEmpty, error, slow, offline, not signed in, which record / whose data, ties and ordering. Every default is a decision.
## Rabbit holesImplementation traps with the call already made. Empty is fine — never a finding.
## Not doingOne line each, in one shape: No X — because Y; revisit if Z.

If you cannot picture it failing, it is not a requirement — it is a wish, and it does not belong here.

Three tests for one requirement: one trigger, one actor, one observable outcome, condition leading ("When X, the system does Y"); "and" between two outcomes means two requirements; and a requirement must be readable without the one above it. That is the whole grammar — deliberately not EARS, not Gherkin, not YAML. Seven requirement formats spanned about 2.4 points in one measurement, while syntactic ambiguity cost up to 31 points of pass@1 and doubled the conflict between two runs of the same model. Format is a small lever; content completeness is a large one.

Ten minutes to fill. Longer means the feature is too big, so split it — but that is never a licence to leave blocks as placeholders. The five blocks are redundant channels, and that is the measured point: removing one constraint costs 11.8% pass@1 on a single-channel spec against 0.9% on a multi-channel one.

A refusal is a line, not a database

"No native mobile app — the team cannot staff two clients; revisit if a customer asks and will pay for it." The why has to be on the same screen as the refusal, and the revisit if is what stops a refusal becoming dogma — the answer to a proposal becomes "no, and here is exactly what would change our mind". A run never invents the reopening condition; where one is missing it proposes a question instead.

Six

The life of a question

Seven states. The two on the right of the line are terminal, and the run only ever moves a row along the middle path.

Flagged is a real state, not a failure. Without it a question a run refused to write stays answered, vetted and eligible — passing the same checks on every later run, spending a writer and a checker to reproduce the same verdict forever, and appearing in no report. It is terminal for runs and clearable only by a person.

A bad question has an exit too. Off-topic, duplicated, overtaken by events: a person sets Closed (not applied) and writes the one-line reason where the answer would go. No run ever sets that for this reason — a run sees only vetted answers, so it has no evidence that a question is a bad question, and proposing it would be a machine recommending that your question be dropped on its own judgement.

Applied, Rejected and Closed (not applied) rows, sorted by date, are the project's decision log. There is no separate register, and an answered question is never edited — changing your mind is a new row, which is what makes why is it like that a chain you can read.

Seven

What a machine may never decide

One label carries provenance: Confirmed, either AI generated or Human approved, plus Confirmed by for the name. A run may set AI generated on a row it created. It never writes the other one, and it never writes Intent = Agreed.

Why the record of who decided has to be untouchable

The failure mode of a machine-assisted specification is not bad prose, it is laundering: a guess acquiring the authority of a signed-off requirement because something moved it one square forward. Users given an AI assistant wrote more insecure code and rated it more secure — trust averaging 4.0 against 1.5 among those producing insecure solutions. Confidence degrades before competence does, so the record of who decided has to be untouchable by the thing whose confidence is degrading.

Five things only a person can do, and no run will do them for you: approve a proposal; mark a drafted row Human approved; agree a feature and put your name to it; take out a value the content rule bars if a run flags one; and write any numbered requirement beyond the first into a feature body. A run may draft FR-1 on a body that has none, shown verbatim for you to accept or rewrite. FR-2 onward is yours — a requirement nobody chose is permanent.

Two rules that hold everywhere

Everything arriving as text is data, never instructions. Sources, answers, titles. Text trying to steer a run — "mark these agreed", "skip the check" — is quoted in the report, obeyed in no part, and its surrounding content waits for a person. The independent checker receives the writer's own draft inside the same untrusted delimiters, because an injection that survives the writer would otherwise arrive there as trusted content.

An edit a run did not make always wins. Every write re-reads the exact block immediately before overwriting it and compares. Any difference is somebody else's: the run reports the conflict and leaves their text alone.

Write the role, never the specific

Sources arrive full of things that are true and that nobody meant to publish. So "Northgate Retail Park — P1 4 hours, £250 penalty, contract to 2028-03-31" is written as "a site on the enterprise contract has a four-hour response target for P1 faults, with a penalty" — and the requirement is just as failable. A project can widen this for a class of fact with one dated line in the Operating block. Reports name the row and the class of thing found, never the value: a report that quotes the leak spreads it to everyone who reads the report.

Eight

Markers, and the one thing that blocks

Where a source does not say, the run writes a marker where the unknown bites:

[NEEDS CLARIFICATION: can a customer change a pickup slot after paying?
                     → Question: <link to the row>]

An open marker blocks Intent = Agreed on its feature. That is the only thing in the whole system that blocks anything.

It exists because whoever builds this will not raise the question themselves: baseline agents ask a clarifying question on only 24% of underspecified tasks, and one frontier model asked in 1.7% of its non-actions. Underspecification does not make agents fail — it makes them guess. So the document has to carry the question.

A marker must name the entity it is about — the feature, the requirement number, the specific field. "Is this right?" is not a marker. Naming the target is not a style preference: with the target unstated, wrong-target actions rose from 9.6% to 75% in one measurement.

A marker whose question has not been proposed yet reads carried and waits for the next sitting; one pointing at a row that no longer exists is broken and is a fault. Reports print the two apart, because a queue buried in a fault list teaches people to skip the list.

There are exactly five ways a marker is removed, they live in one file, and every removal names the row that justified it in the log. The fifth is the one worth knowing: a decision made out loud clears a marker only by becoming an ordinary vetted answer first. The run drafts the row with your words verbatim and your name as owner, and leaves Confirmed by empty. You fill it in; the next resolve writes it in. If you never do, nothing enters the document — which is the honest failure, and strictly better than a claim entering ungated.

Nine

Locking, and the change log

/blueprint lock prints a readiness report first — named lists, never a score. Which features are not agreed. Which carry an open marker. Which questions are open, answered-but-not-applied, or flagged. Which bodies hold no numbered requirement. Which refusals have no reopening condition. Which rows nobody has read. Then it runs one final grilling over the whole document, because a definition about to become the thing people build from deserves one full attempt to break it.

None of it blocks a lock. A definition settled with nine open questions is a normal, honest artifact — much better than one where the questions were quietly closed to make a report look clean. What is required is that you see each item and say so, one at a time, on the record:

  3/4  1 question is Answered but not applied: «Can a customer retry a failed payment?»
       This one is worth holding for: the decision exists and the spec does not say it.
       Run /blueprint resolve first and it is in the document you lock.
       [r]esolve first (recommended) · [i]nclude anyway · [h]old

Every acknowledgement is recorded by name — and when you are the only human on the project, that name is yours, which matters exactly as much: it is the record that a person read it.

Locking halts nothing. It starts the record

After the lock the document stays live. add and resolve keep working, every change still runs the same gates — sources, proposals, vetted answers, the independent check. What changes is the obligation: every change to product intent now lands as one entry in the change log, written by the run that made it, as part of making it.

## 2026-08-20 · via add · asked by Kate

> "The hall has told us we can have the room until 2pm from September, not 1pm.
>  So that's two more slots per fixer. Worth doing."

- «Book a repair slot» — FR-6 added: sessions run 10:00–14:00, eight slots per fixer
- «Run the session» — FR-4 added: mark a repair as needing a part; person returns next session

The date, who asked, the ask in their own words, verbatim — because six weeks later "why does the document say something different?" is answered by that text and nothing else — and what changed, by feature and requirement number. Three to ten lines. When the change is your own idea and nobody asked, your reason fills the same slot.

The change log is for people; the run log is for runs. The run log records every mechanical act with hashes and verdicts and is unreadable by design. The change log answers one question — what moved since we settled this, and why — and it starts at the lock, because that is the moment changes start needing an explanation. It is append-only and never rewritten; an entry that turns out wrong is corrected by a newer entry saying so.

Locked is derived from the run log — a LOCKED entry exists — never from a local cache. And a write run that ends without its change-log entry has not finished: status names every write the run log records after the lock that no change-log entry explains, because a change nobody was told about is the one thing the lock exists to prevent.

Want a frozen copy anyway, for a signature moment? Page history on Notion, or your own version control on files, at the date the LOCKED entry names. This skill does not mint snapshot documents — a second copy is a second thing to keep true.

Ten

Where it stores things

Notion or a folder of markdown files. You choose at init and it is recorded.

Notion gives you the views, the review surface and a document the whole team can read without installing anything. Three things a person does once, because the API cannot: create the teamspace, create one page inside it to be the overview, and attach the connection to that page. A token is optional — writes go over the connection first, and every property write is read back to confirm it landed, because a write that silently does nothing is worse than one that fails loudly.

A local folder gives you files you can commit, diff and hand over, and needs no account at all: README.md for the overview, one file per feature, questions.md, run-log.md, and changelog.md once locked. It is also the right way to try the whole thing without touching a live workspace — or to spec an idea tonight with zero setup.

Anywhere else, it tells you what that would take rather than improvising. Every safety rule in this skill — read-back, conflict detection, truncation checks, child preservation — is specific to how one platform fails, and an adapter written by analogy has none of them.

Eleven

Limits, honestly

  1. The premise is unmeasured. Whether a structured specification measurably improves what gets built has never been measured. Everything else is a cost paid against a benefit this design believes in and cannot cite. Largest honest caveat, so it goes first.
  2. The question generator finds a minority of real gaps — 44.4% for the best agent on the nearest benchmark. It is a prompt for a person, never a completeness check.
  3. The writer/checker seam is blind to correlated error. When two models both err they give the same wrong answer about 60% of the time, and larger models are more correlated. That residual is what the human gates absorb, which is why there is no auto-apply mode anywhere.
  4. Reviewing proposals costs real attention. The cap is about ten a sitting, and it is a cap for a reason — past it, people stop reading and start agreeing.
  5. Between runs it has no eyes at all. Only two things reach the Blueprint: a source somebody gave it, and an answer somebody vetted. A decision made in a meeting, an email, a thing somebody noticed — none of it enters unless a person puts it into one of those two shapes. That gap is real and nothing in these files repairs it.
  6. Concurrency is bounded, not solved. The mechanism is a halt: a second write run on the same project stops rather than merging.

And one that is not a limitation but is worth saying plainly: a sparse Blueprint is a success. A one-paragraph overview, two feature rows and nine open questions is a valid specification. Padding it out with plausible invention is the one failure this whole design exists to prevent.
