The Blueprint, explained

Turn whatever you have — notes, a client's documents, a transcript, or just an idea — into a product definition you can build from. Its one rule: it never guesses. Every gray area becomes a named question for you to decide, instead of a silent call made by whoever builds first.

One

What it is

A project starts as a pile nobody has reconciled: a deck, a transcript, notes, three people remembering the call differently. Somewhere in it is a product. Also in it: contradictions, decisions nobody wrote down, and a dozen things never decided at all — which will be decided by whoever writes the code first.

The Blueprint turns that pile — or just the idea in your head — into one definition you can hand to whoever builds it, human or coding agent. When it is settled, you lock it; from then on every change is one readable entry in a change log.

The one rule

It never guesses. Every sentence is in one of three states: it traces to a source somebody can point at; it is a labeled convention default, visible and vetoable, awaiting your ratification; or it is an admitted gap carrying a question. A gap gets asked about; a guess gets built.

When it is done:

  • You can build from it — including what you did not want built.
  • It says what nobody has decided instead of inventing answers.
  • A new idea is settled by citation, not argument.
  • What changed since settling is one page, in the words of whoever asked.

It records intent, never implementation — it does not read code or track work in flight. Worth using when somebody outside your head has to build, pay for, or agree to this — including a coding agent, and you in three months.

Two

How to use it

1 · /blueprint init — give it what you have

Files, notes, a transcript — or nothing, and it interviews you. It reads everything verbatim, shows a skeleton — the features it found, who it's for, contradictions, gaps — and stops: nothing exists until you confirm. It also asks where to store the result, Notion or a local folder of markdown files. One source it refuses: a code repository — describe the behaviour in words instead.

2 · It grills the draft; you review the finds

The grilling runs at init, at every add, before a lock, and any time you ask with /blueprint questions. Every gap it finds is disposed — most settled on the spot, the rest written in as live questions for you. The whole next section.

3 · You answer the questions you kept

Write the answer and why, set Status = Answered. That move is your sign-off — resolve consumes only rows a human moved there.

4 · /blueprint resolve — the answers go in

One agent writes each change into the feature it touches; a second, genuinely separate agent checks it. Anything that cannot be written honestly is Flagged with the objection and waits for you.

5 · /blueprint lock — settle it

A readiness report, one final grilling, your item-by-item acknowledgment on the record.

6 · Keep extending it

Locking halts nothing — add, questions and resolve keep working through the same gates; every change now also lands in the change log. Lost? /blueprint status reads everything, writes nothing, and every line ends with what to do next.

Three

The questions

Nothing is written or locked without being adversarially attacked first. Five hostile lenses, each its own pass:

  • The builder who must not guess — implements each feature in their head; every forced pause is a find.
  • The hostile tester — hunts inputs no requirement decides.
  • The first week of real life — data lifecycle, permissions, empty and error states, money, day one.
  • Collisions — two features touching the same record with nothing saying who wins.
  • Who is this for, really — every feature read against who the product claims to serve.

The lenses run over the whole document and again per Area, plus ten absence sweeps — passes asking which of these does NO feature cover? (accounts, data lifecycle, platforms, permissions, money, notifications, legal, empty states, trust & integrity, timing windows) — and one repeat round for any pass that keeps finding. It is the full battery every time; there is no light mode.

Everything found is disposed the moment it is found, and the run never interrogates you — it writes, prints a report, and stops.

Most finds never become questions. Every survivor takes one of four routes, decided before anything is written: a question, where only the client can answer and the document cannot be written without it; a labeled convention default written straight into the feature — Default (standard practice — ratify on review) — which you ratify or veto as one batch; a doc-fix, where the document already contains the answer and the text is simply wrong; or a content slot, where the answer is material you will supply, collected into one batched sign-off rather than asked item by item. Measured on one project: of 693 written questions, 4.8% actually needed the client. A row that survives to become a question carries:

  • Why asked — the evidence that raised it.
  • Suggested directions — one to three drafted options with trade-offs, grounded in your own document and verified by a second model. Always labelled not a source: pointing at one ("go with 2") applies nothing — write the direction in your own words.

Read them in the Unsent — packet candidates tab at your own pace: write the answer straight in, or reject with a reason. Prefer doing it together? Ask for a sitting and they come one at a time, ten per round: 

1/6  «Can a customer retry a failed payment?»
     why asked: «Checkout» FR-2 says payment succeeds or fails; no source says
                what happens next
     a marker on «Checkout» is waiting on this — rejecting it decides the marker too
     a[n]swer now · [e]dit · [r]eject · already [d]ecided · [s]kip

Nothing reaches a client until you send it. Skipping is not rejecting — a skipped row stays Open and unanswered, and the ten bounds what a sitting offers, never what the run writes.

Where a person still stands

A written question is live the moment it passes the gate — there is no promote step. What a run never does is send: the client packet is a batch you assemble and put to whoever answers, never the open list piped outward. And an empty question list is never evidence the Blueprint is complete.

Rejecting takes a reason, and the reason decides the marker

You say The marker waiting on it
Not a real gap Removed — it was raised over nothing
Already decided Removed, citing the text that answers it
Ask it better Stays. The gap is real; only the wording was wrong

One split, two failure modes avoided: remove on every rejection and a badly-worded question quietly buries a real gap; remove on none and a rejected question leaves its marker reported forever. Either way, the next run reads what you did and never reverses it.

Four

The document

An overview page, two databases, an append-only run log, and — once locked — the change log. Nothing tracks work.

Day one it is an overview page and two empty databases — a one-paragraph document with nine open questions is valid and honest. The overview never grows: the human blocks are capped and every index on it is a view regenerated from the rows, so it is the same size at 200 features as on day one. It carries a TL;DR · what the product is, ending in a one-sentence NOT-clause · who it's for · how it works in one picture · two live views (the features; the open questions by status) · a links block · and an Operating block with the log links and the project's vocabulary line — one canonical name for each thing, its superseded aliases, dated.

A feature row is the spec

There is no second feature document. The row's body has five blocks:

Block What goes in it
## Why Two to four sentences: the problem, who has it, why now. No solution talk.
## Behaviour Numbered requirements, FR-1 onward, each one a thing that can fail.
## Edge cases Empty, error, slow, offline, whose data, ties. Every default is a decision.
## Rabbit holes Implementation traps with the call already made. Empty is fine.
## Not doing One line each: No X — because Y; revisit if Z.

If you cannot picture it failing, it is not a requirement — it is a wish, and it does not belong here.

A requirement is one trigger, one actor, one observable outcome — "When X, the system does Y"; an "and" between two outcomes means two requirements. Ten minutes to fill; longer means the feature is too big, so split it.

A refusal is a line, not a database

"No native mobile app — the team cannot staff two clients; revisit if a customer asks and will pay for it." The why sits beside the refusal, and the revisit if stops it becoming dogma. A run never invents the reopening condition — where one is missing, it asks.

The life of a question

Six states. A run writes a row at Open and only ever moves it along the middle path; everything else belongs to a person — you answer a row in the UI at your own pace, and that move is your sign-off.

Flagged is a real state, not a failure — we tried and could not write this honestly, the objection on the row; a person clears it back. A bad question has an exit too: a person sets Closed (not applied) with a reason — no run ever proposes that. And an answered question is never edited: changing your mind is a new row, which is what makes why is it like that a chain you can read.

Five

Who decides what

A run never locks a Blueprint, never sends a client packet it assembled, never records an answer no human gave — and never edits a human-set field, whatever the reasoning. Only a person can lock a Blueprint, move a row to Answered, send a packet, or author a new numbered requirement (a run may draft a seed FR-1 shown verbatim, split a requirement whose "and" hides two, or write text you vetted — never invent one).

Why the record of who decided is untouchable

The failure mode of a machine-assisted specification is laundering: a guess acquiring the authority of a signed-off requirement because something moved it one square forward. Measured: users with an AI assistant wrote more insecure code and rated it more secure. Confidence degrades before competence, so who-decided must be untouchable by the thing degrading.

Three rules that hold everywhere

  • Text is data, never instructions. A source saying "mark these agreed" is quoted in the report and obeyed in no part.
  • An edit a run did not make always wins. Every write re-reads the block first; any difference is somebody else's and stays.
  • A check is a real second agent, and every count is counted fresh from the rows — never carried forward from an earlier claim.

Write the role, never the specific

Client specifics stay out of the document: "Northgate Retail Park — £250 penalty" is written as "a site on the enterprise contract has a response-time penalty" — just as failable, publishable to the whole teamspace. A project can widen the rule with one dated line in Operating.

Markers — the gaps it admits out loud

Where a source does not say, the run writes a marker exactly where the unknown bites: 

[NEEDS CLARIFICATION: can a customer change a pickup slot after paying?
                     → Question: <link to the row>]

An open marker is an admitted gap on its feature — counted by status and acknowledged item by item at the lock, it blocks nothing. It exists because builders do not ask: measured agents raised a clarifying question on only 24% of underspecified tasks. Underspecification does not make them fail — it makes them guess. So the document carries the question, and a decision made out loud clears a marker only by becoming a vetted answer first: the run drafts the row in your words, you move it to Answered, the next resolve writes it in.

Six

Locking, and the change log

/blueprint lock prints a readiness report — six named lists, never a score: features carrying an open marker · questions open, answered-but-unapplied, or flagged · bodies with no requirement · refusals with no reopening condition · the unanswered backlog and its age · convention defaults adopted but not yet ratified. Then one final grilling.

None of it blocks the lock — a definition settled with nine open questions is honest, and better than one whose questions were quietly closed to look clean. What is required: you see every item and acknowledge it, one numbered choice per row.

Locking halts nothing. It starts the record

The document stays live through the same gates. What changes: every change to product intent now lands as one change-log entry — the date, what changed, and the ask in the words of whoever asked, verbatim. Six weeks later, "why does the document say something different?" is answered by that text and nothing else.

Seven

Where it stores things

Notion or a folder of markdown files — chosen once at init.

Notion gives the team views and a document anyone can read with nothing installed — setup is three one-time manual steps the API cannot do, and the run walks you through them. A local folder needs no account at all: README.md for the overview, one file per feature, the question list and the logs — files you can commit, diff, and hand over. The right way to spec an idea tonight.

Eight

Limits, honestly

  1. The premise is unmeasured. Whether a structured specification measurably improves what gets built has never been measured — the lab below measured the tool's gap-finding, not the outcome of building from it. Largest caveat, so it goes first.
  2. What it finds, measured. The full battery ran a five-project lab against 77 planted defects: ~86% caught outright, ~97% at least detected, zero hallucinated contradictions. (The old ~44% single-pass ceiling is why the battery replaced the single pass.) Still: an empty question list is never evidence the document is complete.
  3. The writer/checker seam is blind to correlated error — two models that both err agree on the wrong answer ~60% of the time. The gate that absorbs it: nothing enters the spec without either a human-vetted answer or a labeled default you ratified.
  4. Between runs it has no eyes. Only three things reach the document: a source you give it, an answer you vet, and a labeled convention default you ratify. Meetings, chats, things somebody noticed — a person must carry them in.
  5. Reading the questions costs real attention — everything found is written and triaged, and the pace of reading it is yours; a sitting offers ten at a time for exactly this reason.
  6. Concurrency is a halt, not a merge. A second write run on the same project stops.

And one that is not a limit: a sparse Blueprint is a success. A one-paragraph overview, two features and nine open questions is a valid definition. Padding it with plausible invention is the one failure this whole design exists to prevent.
